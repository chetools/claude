Spinning, orbiting cylinder in a box

A cylinder spins, orbits the centre of a closed square box, or both, and stirs the fluid inside. The incompressible Navier–Stokes equations are solved by finite volumes, live, on your graphics card (WebGPU compute shaders). Raise the Reynolds number to go from steady, laminar flow, through unsteady vortex shedding, to chaotic two-dimensional turbulence. No turbulence model is imposed: the flow is resolved directly where the grid allows, and the advection scheme supplies the sub-grid dissipation where it doesn't (Theory §8). A Python/numpy reference explains the method step by step and checks the GPU results value by value.

Flow particles are massless tracers carried by the computed velocity. Each draws its path as a fading trail, so the trails point along the flow and are longer and darker where the fluid moves faster. They are pathlines, where fluid has been, which in unsteady flow differ from the instantaneous streamlines (Theory §9). Particles that age, leave the box or enter the cylinder are re-seeded at random.
The grey disc is the cylinder; the line inside it turns with the spin. The small × marks the fixed velocity probe whose spectrum is plotted on the right. An orbiting cylinder passes the probe once per orbit, so peaks at the orbit frequency (dotted line) and its harmonics are forced, even in laminar flow. A new peak at an unrelated frequency means the flow has bifurcated, for example to vortex shedding. A broad, continuous spectrum means chaos. Moving a slider changes the running flow; changing the grid size restarts it.

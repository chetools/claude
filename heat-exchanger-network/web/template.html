Matches: forbidden pairs, materials and penalty factors

Each cell is one hot/cold pair. Forbidden is a hard constraint. "auto" picks the cheapest shell/tube pair both fluids tolerate (both touch the tubes; the shell suits the shell-side fluid). The factor multiplies the exchanger cost, e.g. for a double tube sheet on a hazardous-but-allowed pair. Grey cells cannot be matched.

The classic sequential method, one step at a time. Each step takes the result of the previous one.

Step 1 — LP: utility targets

Step 2 — MILP: fewest matches

Step 3 — NLP: network and loads

Each MILP match gets its own stage, ordered by the mean temperature of its heat; Rust then optimises the loads (log-barrier + L-BFGS) and a greedy search moves single matches between stages and switches utilities on/off.

Click a hot stream inside a stage, then a cold stream in the same stage to place a match; click a match to remove it; click a utility circle to change or switch it off. Forbidden or incompatible partners turn grey once a hot stream is selected. After every change Rust optimises the loads and the TAC updates.

Simulated annealing over the stage-wise superstructure (Yee & Grossmann form). Every move adds, removes or moves one match or changes one utility; each candidate's loads are optimised by the NLP. Several independent chains run in parallel Web Workers; after each epoch all chains restart from the best network so far at a lower temperature.

WebGPU batch evaluation

Could a GPU help? The annealing itself is sequential (each NLP is an L-BFGS run), so the only GPU-friendly part is evaluating many candidate load vectors of one topology at once. This measures that batch (TAC of N random load vectors of the current best network) on WebGPU (float32) against the CPU workers (float64), and checks that the two agree.
